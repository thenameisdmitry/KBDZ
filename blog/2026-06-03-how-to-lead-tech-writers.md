When I was promoted to Team Lead in 2021, I faced a challenge familiar to many first-time managers: how do you balance leadership responsibilities with your own day-to-day work without becoming a bottleneck?

Management occupied roughly 20–30% of my time. My focus was on building a sustainable documentation operation rather than simply supervising people. We introduced DITA-based practices in our XML-driven MadCap Flare environment, standardized terminology and writing conventions, and established quarterly quality reviews. Most importantly, I wanted the team to improve continuously while maintaining ownership of their work.

![lead-tech-writers](/img/blog/article-lead-tech-writers/lead-tech-writers-1.png)

## Being a leader is simple, being an effective leader is hard

My leadership philosophy was simple: technical writers are professionals, not content-producing machines. Reviews should highlight opportunities for improvement rather than dwell on mistakes. When people feel trusted and supported, they are far more likely to raise their own standards.

A good example involved one of our writers whose drafts frequently required multiple review cycles due to typos and inaccuracies in business examples. Instead of focusing on the symptoms, we addressed the process. I introduced a simple workflow diagram with an additional self-review step, encouraged earlier preparation of business examples during planning, and introduced Grammarly as a final quality check.

The solution was simple but effective. The writer spent slightly more time reviewing drafts before submission, yet the number of review iterations dropped significantly. Documentation quality improved, reviewers regained valuable time, and the writer became more consistent because fewer documents were returned for rework.

## Real test

The real test came in 2022 when I had to rebuild the documentation team after the company's relocation to the United Kingdom. Different people, same principles: clear workflows, professional standards, continuous improvement, and respect for the people behind the process. Within six months, the team's output increased fourfold compared to the early stages of rebuilding.

Looking back, I learned that effective leadership is not about personally solving every problem. It is about creating an environment where talented people can do their best work. Strong processes matter. Quality standards matter. But the real multiplier is a team that trusts the workflow, trusts its leader, and takes pride in the result.
