If you browse the GitHub long enough, you'll notice that while almost every repository has a README file, comprehensive guides remain surprisingly rare.

The reason is simple. The primary goal of documentation is to educate people about a product: what it does, why it exists, how to use it efficiently, how to avoid mistakes, and how to get the most value from it. Creating good documentation requires time, effort, discipline, and a genuine desire to help others succeed with your product. Those who neglect documentation usually have other priorities, and educating users is not one of them.

In the enterprise world, however, documentation is not optional. Whether we're talking about Fintech platforms, treasury systems, SaaS products, or enterprise software in general, documentation is one of the foundations upon which customer success is built.

<!-- truncate -->

Consider a simple analogy. When you purchase a sophisticated piece of equipment (a coffee maker, a water boiler, or perhaps a new piece of home electronics) you usually dive in the manual first. There are two obvious reasons for this. First, you don't want to break something expensive through trial and error. Second, you want to start using it as quickly and efficiently as possible.

The same principle applies to fintech products. Customers want exactly the same things. First, they do not want to lose money. Second, they want to generate more value from the product they purchased. Good documentation helps achieve both goals.

Strictly speaking, customers can judge the maturity and class of a product simply by looking at the quality of its documentation. The line between "good" and "bad" documentation is very clear, yet remarkably hard to reach. In my experience, great documentation rests on three pillars: *Integrity, Actionability, and Beauty.*

![three-pillars1](/img/blog/article-three-pillars/three-pillars-1.png)

### ✅ Integrity

This one almost goes without saying.

If your documentation provides incorrect information, omits critical details, or misleads users, you can kiss your credibility goodbye.

Integrity is not achieved by accident. It requires continuous research, attention to detail, close collaboration with experts, participation in testing activities, thorough reviews, and an ability to react to issues at a speed of light before issues become customer problems. Ultimately, documentation integrity is the result of an efficient workflow.

Clients rarely notice accurate documentation. They simply expect it. However, they notice inaccurate documentation immediately - and then they become very tempted to add an "ex-" to the word "client". 

Once trust is lost, winning it back becomes a difficult task.

### ✅ Actionability

Technical documentation is not a novel.

Nobody opens a knowledge base for entertainment. People arrive with a problem and expect to leave with a solution. The best documentation respects the reader's time. It is focused, practical, and designed to help users accomplish a specific goal with minimal effort. No one wants to navigate through a dozen paragraphs of background information just to discover the one step they actually need.

This is where empathy becomes one of the most valuable skills a technical writer can possess. A strong technical writer constantly looks at documentation through the customer's eyes. They identify friction points, unclear explanations, missing examples, and unnecessary complexity.

Mediocre documentation explains. Great documentation enables action.

### ✅ Beauty

I deliberately use the word "beauty" instead of "clear interface" or "intuitive design" because true beauty in documentation encompasses all of those things and then some.

An exceptional knowledge base is beautifully structured, beautifully written, beautifully designed, and beautifully delivered. Users instinctively know where to go. They find answers quickly. Navigation feels natural, and their experience feels effortless.

Beauty in documentation is not decoration, but a winning point amongst competition. So many companies treat their documentation just as a compliance checkbox: we don't really care about it, but it has to exist because customers expect it to exist. Winners treat sources of knowledge differently. They understand that documentation is part of the product experience itself.

When users interact with your knowledge base, they are still interacting with your product, just through a different interface or access point.

### Final Thoughts

Of course, these three principles do not capture the entire discipline of technical writing. Documentation also depends on maintenance, stakeholder collaboration, governance, localization, analytics, and now - AI-assisted workflows.

Yet without **Integrity**, **Actionability**, and **Beauty**, everything else rests on shaky foundations.

Before I finish, I'd like to offer my own analogy.

Imagine attending a product presentation for a solution you're considering purchasing.

The lights dim. The screen comes alive. The presenter walks onto the stage.

She greets the audience with confident polite smile and quickly gets to the point. She highlights the most important features, demonstrates practical use cases, answers every question, and never wastes a minute of your time. Her presentation is clear, engaging, informative, and effortless to follow. As a cherry on top, her looks are breathtaking, her voice is warm, her eyes are clever, her outfit is top-notch.

By the end of the presentation you are in love: both in the product, and in the presenter.

Exceptional documentation creates the same effect.

It becomes a place that customers turn to first because experience has taught them that answers can be found there quickly and reliably. When your documentation reaches that level, your support team may discover the most pleasant problem of all:

*There are very few tickets left to resolve.*
